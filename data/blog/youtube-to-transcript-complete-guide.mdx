## How It Actually Works

YouTube already stores captions for most videos — either uploaded by the creator or generated automatically using [YouTube's own automatic captioning system](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554). A converter doesn't create new text; it reads the captions that already exist and reformats them into something you can copy, search or export. That's also why a video with no captions at all can't produce a transcript — there's nothing to extract.

## How Accurate Is a YouTube Transcript?

It depends entirely on the source:

- **Manually created captions** (added by the video's creator) are usually very accurate, since a person wrote or reviewed them.
- **Auto-generated captions** (YouTube's own speech-to-text) are generally good for clear, single-speaker audio in common languages, but can misread names, technical terms, heavy accents or overlapping speakers.

If you need publication-grade accuracy — for a direct quote in an article, for example — treat the transcript as a very strong first draft and spot-check anything that matters against the video.

## Choosing a Format

Different formats suit different jobs:

- **Plain text (TXT)** — best for reading, note-taking, or pasting into an AI tool for summarization.
- **Timestamped TXT** — best when you need to know *when* something was said, for citations or building chapter markers.
- **[SRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SubRip)** — the standard subtitle format, best when you're feeding the transcript into a video editor or media player.

You don't have to pick just one — a good converter generates all three from a single result.

## YouTube to Transcript vs. YouTube's Built-In Panel

YouTube does expose transcripts natively (⋯ → Show transcript under any video), and it's worth knowing the tradeoff:

- **Setup** — a converter is a paste-a-link step; YouTube's panel needs opening the video and clicking through a menu.
- **Clean copy-paste** — a converter gives you clean text; YouTube's panel mixes a timestamp into every single line.
- **Downloadable file** — a converter exports TXT, timestamped TXT and SRT; YouTube's panel offers no download at all.
- **Works away from the video page** — a converter works from just the URL; YouTube's panel only works on the video page itself.

The built-in panel is fine if you just want to read along in the moment. A converter is the better choice the moment you want to *do something* with the text.
